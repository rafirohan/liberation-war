Nine months that changed South Asia

A detailed chronology of the 1971 Bangladesh Liberation War, from political crisis to independence. Each entry links to source documents, eyewitness accounts, and historical records held in our archive.

The Path to Independence

The 1971 Liberation War emerged from decades of political tension between West Pakistan and the Bengali-majority East Pakistan. Following the 1970 elections, in which the Awami League won an overwhelming majority, the Pakistani military launched Operation Searchlight on 25 March 1971 to suppress Bengali nationalism. Nine months of war, genocide, and struggle followed—ending with the creation of an independent Bangladesh.

1940–1970

Political foundation

Following Partition in 1947, East Pakistan becomes increasingly marginalized within the Pakistani state. The movement for Bengali language rights (1952), the Six-Point Program (1966), and growing resentment over economic disparity lay the foundation for independence movements.

7 March 1971

Sheikh Mujibur Rahman's historic speech

At a mass gathering on the Race Course Ground in Dhaka, Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, leader of the Awami League, delivers a speech calling on Bengalis to prepare for a freedom struggle. His words—"This time the struggle is for our freedom"—become the rallying cry of the independence movement.

→ Race Course Ground address transcript
25–26 March 1971

Operation Searchlight begins

The Pakistani military launches Operation Searchlight, a crackdown to suppress the independence movement. Attacks target Dhaka University, student hostels, and civilian neighborhoods. In the early hours of 26 March, amid the violence, independence is declared—officially marking the beginning of the Liberation War.

→ Declaration of Independence, 26 March 1971
March–April 1971

First armed resistance

The Mukti Bahini (Liberation Army) forms to resist Pakistani occupation. Fighting breaks out across East Pakistan. Border regions see early military engagements as the movement transitions from political protest to armed struggle. Thousands of freedom fighters cross into India for training and regrouping.

→ Freedom fighters' testimonies
17 April 1971

Mujibnagar Government proclaimed

At Baidyanathtala in Meherpur district, the provisional government of Bangladesh is sworn in. Sheikh Mujibur Rahman is designated President (in absentia, imprisoned by Pakistan), and Syed Nazrul Islam becomes Vice-President. This government internationally represents the independence movement.

→ Mujibnagar Proclamation of Independence
April–July 1971

Genocide and mass atrocities

Pakistani forces conduct systematic killings targeting intellectuals, political activists, and the general population. Estimates of deaths range from 300,000 to 3 million. Mass rape is used as a weapon of war. The humanitarian crisis displaces millions, creating a refugee exodus into India.

→ Genocide press dispatches and reports
June–August 1971

International diplomatic campaign

The Mujibnagar government conducts an intensive diplomatic mission to secure international recognition. Letters exchanged with world leaders seek support for refugee relief and political resolution. The Blood Telegram from U.S. diplomats in Dhaka criticizes American policy toward Pakistan.

→ Diplomatic correspondence, 1971
9 August 1971

Indo-Soviet Treaty signed

India and the Soviet Union sign a twenty-year Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation. The treaty signals Soviet support for India's potential military intervention and strengthens the security framework ahead of the war's final phase.

→ Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation
August–December 1971

Intensified guerrilla warfare

Mukti Bahini operations expand across all sectors. Coordinated attacks on military installations, supply lines, and Pakistani forces increase. Atrocities continue as Pakistani forces attempt to consolidate control. By December, the Mukti Bahini has grown to over 100,000 fighters.

→ Freedom fighters' accounts of combat
3–16 December 1971

Joint Forces offensive and Pakistani surrender

India officially enters the war, opening a western front while the Mukti Bahini launches coordinated offensives. The Joint Forces—composed of Indian troops and Mukti Bahini fighters—rapidly advance on Dhaka. On 16 December, Pakistani forces surrender at the Race Course Ground. Bangladesh is born.

→ Instrument of Surrender, 16 December 1971
16 December 1971

Bijoy Dibosh — Victory Day

Pakistan formally surrenders to the combined forces of India and the Mukti Bahini. General Niazi signs the Instrument of Surrender before General Jacob of India and representatives of the Mujibnagar government. Bangladesh's independence is internationally recognized. The nation's first flag is raised over Dhaka.

→ Photographs from Victory Day celebrations
10 January 1972

Sheikh Mujibur Rahman returns to Dhaka

After his release by Pakistan, Sheikh Mujibur Rahman returns to a liberated Bangladesh. He is welcomed as the nation's founding leader. The Awami League-led government begins the work of rebuilding the war-devastated nation and establishing democratic institutions.

→ Photographs of national celebrations
1972

Simla Agreement signed

India and Pakistan sign the bilateral Simla Agreement, resolving outstanding post-war issues. The accord recognizes Bangladesh and establishes the framework for normalized relations between India and Pakistan.

→ Simla Agreement

Legacy and remembrance

The 1971 Liberation War remains central to Bangladesh's national identity. Approximately 3 million people died, millions were displaced, and the nation's infrastructure was devastated. Yet from that tragedy emerged a free nation, born from the sacrifice and determination of ordinary Bengalis.

Today, 16 December is observed as Bijoy Dibosh (Victory Day), a national holiday celebrating independence. Memorials, museums, and digital archives preserve the memory of those who fought and died. Educational programs ensure that younger generations understand the cost of freedom.

This archive is part of that ongoing work of remembrance and documentation—ensuring that the voices, documents, and images of 1971 are preserved for research, education, and reflection by future generations.
